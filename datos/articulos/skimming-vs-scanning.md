# Skimming vs Scanning: Differences and When to Use Each

People often use "skimming" and "scanning" as if they were the same thing. They are not. Both mean moving through text faster than you normally would, but they answer different questions. Picking the wrong one is a common reason people feel they "read fast but remember nothing" or "searched the page and still missed the answer."

## The short answer

- **Skimming** is for the **gist**. You want to know what a text is about and whether it deserves a slower read.
- **Scanning** is for a **specific item**. You already know what you are looking for, such as a name, number or date, and you want to find it.

A University of Warwick study-skills page defines skimming as "getting a quick impression or general overview of a book or article." It defines scanning as reading "used to obtain specific information from a piece of text." Its examples: skim a textbook page to decide whether it is useful, and scan to find a particular number in a telephone directory ([Warwick](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/ces/research/centre/research_opportunities/jie/rs/sub1)).

## Skimming vs scanning at a glance

| | Skimming | Scanning |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Understand the overall idea | Find one specific piece of information |
| You know beforehand | Roughly nothing, or only the topic | Exactly what you are looking for |
| Typical text | Article, chapter, report, email | Timetable, index, contract, documentation, search results |
| What you read | Titles, first sentences, conclusions | Only the area around your keyword |
| Output | "What is this about? Is it relevant?" | "The answer is on this line" |
| Main risk | Missing nuance and detail | Missing context around the match |

## When to skim

Skim when the decision is "should I read this properly?" Some examples:

- You have five articles open for a project and need to decide which two matter.
- You are standing in a bookshop or library and want to check whether a non-fiction book covers what you need.
- You are reviewing a long email thread before replying.

A practical way to skim (my suggestion, not a research-backed method):

1. Read the title, headings and any summary or conclusion.
2. Read the first sentence of each paragraph.
3. Stop and write down, in one sentence, what the text is about. If you can't, you have not skimmed it yet, or the text is not clear.

## When to scan

Scan when you know the question. Some examples:

- Finding the check-out time in a hotel confirmation.
- Looking up a clause in a contract.
- Finding the one parameter you need on a long documentation page.

A practical way to scan (again my suggestion):

1. Decide on the exact keyword or pattern first: a name, number, unit or word.
2. Use the page's own structure (headings, bold terms, an index) to jump near it.
3. Once you find it, read the surrounding sentence or two. A number without its context is easy to misread.

On a screen, the browser's find function (Ctrl+F or Cmd+F) is scanning done for you.

## Using both together

The two combine well. The Warwick page notes the sequence: skim first to judge whether a text is relevant, then scan for the specific details you need ([Warwick](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/ces/research/centre/research_opportunities/jie/rs/sub1)). For example, skim a research report's abstract and headings to see if it fits your topic. If it does, scan for the sample size or the date.

## What skimming costs you

Skimming is useful, but it is not a free way to read faster. A 2016 review in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* looked at the evidence on speed reading. Its abstract says "there is a trade-off between speed and accuracy," and that it is unlikely readers can double or triple their speed without losing comprehension. It adds that speed reading or skimming can get you through a text faster with moderate comprehension, and that "language skill is at the heart of reading speed" ([Psychological Science in the Public Interest](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100615623267)).

A Psychology Today post discussing the same review quotes it directly. Skimming "is an important skill and may be a reasonable way to cope with the overwhelming amount of text we have to read," but "any gains in speed through such techniques will be offset by concomitant losses in comprehension" ([Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/reading-minds/201710/so-much-to-read-so-little-time)).

In practice, this means:

- Use skimming for triage, not for texts you will be tested on or need to act on carefully.
- Don't judge a technique by how fast you got through the page. Judge it by what you can accurately say afterwards.

## Where RSVP fits

RSVP (rapid serial visual presentation) is a different thing from both. It shows one word at a time in a fixed spot, so your eyes don't move across the line. You are still meant to read every word, so it is neither skimming nor scanning. The same Psychology Today post describes RSVP as showing "only one word at a time in a restricted viewing box at a predetermined speed," and says that because it blocks backward eye movements, "comprehension invariably suffers," especially for texts longer than a single sentence ([Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/reading-minds/201710/so-much-to-read-so-little-time)).

That is a useful caution. It makes RSVP a poor fit for scanning, since you can't jump to a spot, and a questionable one for dense material where you often need to re-read. If you want to try it, use it for continuous text, set a speed you can still follow, and check yourself afterwards. Write two sentences on what you just read and compare with the text.

## A simple self-test

Whichever method you use, test it before trusting it:

1. Pick an article of around 1,000 words that you haven't read.
2. Skim it for one minute and write down the main point and two supporting points.
3. Read it normally and compare.

If your notes miss the main point, skimming alone isn't enough for that kind of text. This is an informal check, not a scientific measure.
